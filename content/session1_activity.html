Four ways to decide

A manager deciding how many milk pouches to send, or which districts to launch a cooler in, can lean on four kinds of support. They are not rivals. Each one costs more time and data than the one before it, and each one is stronger where the one before it is weak.

Terminology Alert: analytics continuum. A way of describing how much evidence a decision rests on, from personal judgement at one end to models learned from large amounts of data at the other. Most real decisions use more than one point on it.
1

IntuitionWhat an experienced person believes, built from years of seeing the business. Fast and free, works when there is no data, but it is easily fooled by memory and recent events.

2

ResearchEvidence somebody else has already gathered: census figures, industry reports, published studies, what competitors did. Cheap, broad, but not about your own customers.

3

AnalysisStudying your own past data to see what actually happened: sales by district, orders by hour, late payments by customer type. Specific to your business, but it looks backwards.

4

Machine learningA model that learns patterns from that data and makes a prediction for each new case, at a scale no person could handle. Powerful, but only for questions it was built and tested on.

Real world examples of how decisions are made across the analytics continuum

  • Intuition set the direction at Zillow: two founders believed home prices should be public, long before any data proved it.
  • Research told Godrej how many households live in each district and how hot the summers are, which no internal sales report can say.
  • Analysis showed Swiggy that late deliveries cluster between 7 pm and 10 pm and on rainy days.
  • Machine learning then gave every single order its own delivery estimate, which no team of people could do by hand.

The lesson of the cases is not that machine learning wins. It is that a decision should rest on the highest point of the continuum the question can actually support, and that human judgement stays in the loop at every point.

What to take away

1. Data usually beats gut feel, but not always

Across the cases, moving from judgement to data improved decisions: Swiggy's delivery promise, Amul's dispatch quantity, IDFC FIRST Bank's collection calls, Godrej's launch districts. The gains came where the same kind of decision is made again and again, and where the result is recorded so the model can learn from it.

2. Intuition holds its ground in three places

  • When there is no relevant past data: a brand new product, a new market, an event nobody has seen before.
  • When the situation has just changed in a way the data cannot know: a competitor collapses, a flood shuts a highway, a rule changes overnight.
  • When the question is about values rather than prediction: whether flagging a customer is fair, whether a price is decent, whether a launch is worth the risk.

3. A model is only as good as the question it was tested on

Zillow's estimate was accurate enough to attract millions of visitors and far too rough to buy houses with the company's own money. Same model, different question, opposite outcome.

4. Keep people in the loop

Zillow's pricing experts used to adjust the algorithm's offers. When they were told to stop, errors went straight into purchases. The strongest arrangement is not people or models, but people with models.
