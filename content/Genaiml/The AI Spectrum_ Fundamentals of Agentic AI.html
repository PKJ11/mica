Fundamentals of Agentic AI: a class activity

The AI Spectrum

Six rounds that take you from “what is an agent?” to deciding how much freedom to give one. Every round checks itself, so you’ll know what you’ve got right as you go.

    Round 1 of 6

    Place each system on the AI spectrum

    The idea

    Not every AI system is an agent. AI systems sit on an AI spectrum, and one question decides where each one sits: who decides the next step?

    At the left end, people decide every step in advance. At the right end, the AI is given a goal and decides its own steps to reach it. An agent is an AI system that pursues a goal by choosing its own actions, using tools, and checking its own progress.

    Tap a level button on each card to send it there, or drag the card. Place all 12, then check your answers.

    Round 2 of 6

    Take an agent apart

    The idea

    An agent isn’t one thing. It’s a model (the reasoning engine) wrapped in parts that give it direction, information, hands and limits.

    Meet the campaign agent at Kesar & Co., a made-up sweet-shop chain in Ahmedabad. It has been set up to run the Diwali pre-order campaign. Each card describes one part of it.

    Sort the 12 cards into the eight parts of an agent. Watch for the difference between knowledge and memory.

    Round 3 of 6

    Read the agent’s work log

    The idea

    Agents work in a loop. They think about the goal, act using a tool, observe what happened, and think again. The loop repeats until the goal is met, or until a person needs to step in.

    The ability to change the plan based on what it observes is what separates an agent from a workflow.

    Think
    Reasoning or planning what to do next
    Act
    Using a tool to do something
    Observe
    Reading the result of an action
    Ask a human
    Pausing for a person to decide
    Finish
    Goal met, wrapping up and stopping

    Round 4 of 6

    When one agent isn’t enough

    The idea

    Big jobs are often split across several agents, each with a narrow role. Narrow roles are easier to instruct, test and fix, in the same way a team of specialists beats one person doing everything.

    There are five common ways to arrange a team of agents. The difference that trips people up: in fan-out the split is decided in advance, while a manager decides the split as it goes.

    Part A. Name the arrangement

    Sort the 10 cards into the five arrangements.

    Part B. How agents plug in

    The idea

    Agents need standard connections, the way phones need standard chargers. Two open standards matter. MCP (Model Context Protocol) connects an agent to tools and data, such as Google Drive, a CRM or a calendar. A2A (Agent2Agent) lets one agent work with another agent, even one built by a different company.

    Sort the 6 cards: is this an agent reaching a tool, or an agent talking to another agent?

    Round 5 of 6

    Diagnose what went wrong

    The idea

    Autonomy multiplies mistakes. A chatbot’s error is one bad answer that you can ignore. An agent’s error can repeat, spread to other systems, or go public before anyone notices.

    The good news: every common failure has a design fix. Builders don’t hope agents behave; they design so that mistakes are caught.

    For each incident, pick the failure. Then read the fix. You get one try per incident.

    Round 6 of 6

    Make the design call

    The idea

    Good builders choose the least autonomy that reliably gets the job done. More autonomy costs more, is harder to test and has more ways to fail. Add it only when the task truly needs it.

    When you do build an agent, give it a clear goal and put people at the points that matter: anything public, costly or hard to undo.

    Make eight design decisions. You get one try per question.

    Debrief

    What you now know

    1. An agent is an AI system given a goal, not a list of steps. It chooses its own actions, uses tools, checks its progress and decides when it’s done.
    2. AI systems sit on an AI spectrum. Rules automation, chatbot, copilot, AI workflow, agent, multi-agent system. The deciding question is who chooses the next step.
    3. A workflow is not an agent. In a workflow, people fixed the steps and AI does the work inside them. An agent changes its plan based on what it observes.
    4. An agent has eight parts. A goal, instructions, a model, tools, knowledge, memory, guardrails and human checkpoints.
    5. Knowledge is looked up; memory is built up. Knowledge is reference material. Memory is what the agent carries forward from earlier in a task or from past tasks.
    6. Agents run a loop. Think, act, observe, and repeat, pausing to ask a human when needed and finishing when the goal is met.
    7. Teams of agents follow a few arrangements. Relay, router, fan-out, manager and team, and maker and checker.
    8. Agents plug in through standards. MCP connects an agent to tools and data. A2A connects an agent to other agents.
    9. Autonomy multiplies mistakes, and each failure has a fix. Making things up, endless loops, prompt injection, missing checkpoints, too much access, lost instructions, sprawl and missing logs.
    10. Choose the least autonomy that works. Give agents specific goals and limits, and keep people in charge of anything public, costly or hard to undo.

    Discuss with your partner

    • Pick one task from your own internship or work. Where does it sit on the AI spectrum today, and where could it sit?
    • If you built that agent, which one failure from Round 5 worries you most, and what’s your fix?
    • Where exactly would you put the human checkpoint, and why there?
