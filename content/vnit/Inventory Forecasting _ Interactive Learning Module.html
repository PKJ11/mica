    Start Chapter 1: Nodes and flows

    Chapter 1

    Supply chain: nodes and flows

    A supply chain is the network of businesses and places through which a product reaches the customer. Before we plan any inventory, we must know where stock is kept and who orders from whom.

    Nodes and links

    A node is a point in the supply chain where stock is made, stored or sold: a factory, a warehouse, a distributor's godown or a shop. A link connects two nodes: the truck route, the order process and the payment terms between them. Inventory sits at nodes, and it also sits on links while it is being transported.

    The toor dal chain has the following nodes:

    • Manufacturer: Latur Dal Mill buys raw toor from farmers and APMC traders, cleans it, splits it into dal and packs it in 1 kg packs.
    • C&F agent (Carrying and Forwarding agent): Arjun C&F Agent keeps the mill's stock in our region and dispatches it to distributors as per the mill's instructions. The stock still belongs to the mill. Arjun earns a commission for storing and handling it.
    • Distributor: Kavya Distributors buys stock from the mill, keeps it in its own godown, and sells it to large retailers and to stockists on credit.
    • Stockist: Nithansh Stockists buys smaller lots from Kavya Distributors and supplies kirana shops in nearby towns.
    • Warehouse (distribution centre): Madavi Warehouse is Dhanvanshi's own warehouse. It buys from Kavya Distributors and supplies all three Dhanvanshi branches.
    • Retailer: the three Dhanvanshi Super Store branches sell to the final customer.
    • Customer: the household that buys the pack. All demand in the chain starts here.

    Three flows

    Three things move along the links. Material (the dal itself) moves downstream, from the mill towards the customer. Information (orders, sales data, stock reports) mostly moves upstream, from the customer side towards the mill. Money also moves upstream, as each buyer pays its supplier. Use the buttons to switch between the flows, and click any node to read about it.

      Where is Niharika Logistics?

      Niharika Logistics is not a node. It does not buy or sell the dal; it moves it from one node to the next. While the dal is on a Niharika truck, it is still inventory. It is called pipeline (or in-transit) stock, and it belongs to whoever owns the goods during transit.

      Upstream and downstream

      For Madavi Warehouse, Kavya Distributors, Arjun C&F Agent and the mill are upstream (the supply side). The three branches and the customers are downstream (the demand side). Every planning decision at a node depends on how fast its upstream partner can supply and how much its downstream partner will ask for.

      Chapter 2

      Logistics and distribution planning

      Logistics is the work of moving and storing goods so that the right quantity reaches the right node at the right time, at a reasonable cost.

      Parts of logistics

      • Inbound logistics: goods coming into a node. For Madavi Warehouse, this is the dal coming from Kavya Distributors.
      • Outbound logistics: goods going out of a node. For Madavi Warehouse, this is the stock sent to the three branches.
      • Warehousing: receiving, checking, storing, picking and dispatching stock. At receipt, the warehouse prepares a Goods Receipt Note (GRN) after checking the quantity and quality.
      • Reverse logistics: goods moving back up the chain, such as damaged packs, near-expiry stock and returns.

      Full truck load or part truck load?

      In India, most consumer goods move by road. A transporter such as Niharika Logistics offers two options. In a full truck load (FTL), one customer books the whole truck and pays a fixed amount per trip, whatever the load. In a part truck load (PTL), the customer pays per kg, and the transporter combines loads of several customers in one truck. PTL is cheaper for small loads, but usually takes one or two extra days because goods are sorted at the transporter's hub.

      Niharika Logistics rates: FTL (9-tonne truck) ₹18,000 per trip. PTL ₹3 per kg.

      Break-even load between FTL and PTLBreak-even load = FTL rate per tripPTL rate per kg = 18,0003 = 6,000 kg

      Network design: hub and spoke

      Dhanvanshi does not ask Kavya Distributors to deliver to each branch separately. Kavya delivers to Madavi Warehouse (the hub), and the warehouse sends stock to the branches (the spokes). This gives larger, cheaper inbound deliveries and one place to hold safety stock for all branches. The warehouse sends one truck on a milk run, a fixed route that visits Gandhi Chowk, Station Road and Civil Lines in one trip, instead of three separate trucks.

      Distribution planning

      Distribution planning decides how much stock each branch should get, and when. Each branch sends a daily indent (request) to the warehouse. The warehouse adds up the branch requirements to decide what it must order from Kavya Distributors. When the warehouse has enough stock, every branch gets what it asked for. When stock is short, the planner must decide who gets how much. The simple measure used for this is days of cover.

      Days of coverDays of cover = Stock in handAverage daily demand

      Allocation exercise

      A Kavya Distributors delivery is late, so today's milk run can carry only 250 packs of toor dal. The next truck will reach the branches after 4 days. Split the 250 packs among the three branches and see how long each branch can last.

      Fair share allocation

      Add the stock at all branches to the stock available for dispatch: 60 + 90 + 20 + 250 = 420 packs. Total daily demand is 55 + 40 + 25 = 120 packs, so the system can cover 420 ÷ 120 = 3.5 days. Fair share gives each branch enough to reach 3.5 days of cover. All branches then run short at about the same time, instead of one branch running out early while another has extra stock.

      Chapter 3

      Inventory: types and costs

      Inventory is stock kept for future use or sale. Every node holds some inventory, because supply and demand never match exactly day by day.

      Types of inventory by stage

      At Latur Dal Mill, raw toor bought from the mandi is raw material. Toor that has been cleaned and split but not yet packed is work in progress (WIP). Packed 1 kg packs are finished goods. For Kavya Distributors, Madavi Warehouse and the branches, everything they hold is finished goods (also called merchandise).

      Types of inventory by purpose

      TypeWhy it existsToor dal example
      Cycle stockWe order in lots, not one pack at a time, so stock is used up gradually between two deliveries.Madavi Warehouse receives 1,980 packs and uses them over about 16 days.
      Safety stockProtects against demand that is higher than forecast, or deliveries that come late.89 packs kept as a buffer at Madavi Warehouse.
      Pipeline stockStock that has been dispatched but not yet received.Dal on a Niharika Logistics truck between Kavya Distributors and Madavi Warehouse.
      Anticipation stockBuilt up in advance for a known rise in demand, such as a festival or a price increase.Extra stock bought in September for Dussehra and Diwali.
      Dead stockStock that is not selling, is damaged, or is close to expiry.Packs with torn seals, or a slow pack size that nobody buys.

      Costs of inventory

      • Ordering cost (S): the cost of placing and receiving one order, such as raising the purchase order, booking the truck, unloading, quality check and preparing the GRN. It does not depend on the quantity ordered. For Madavi Warehouse, it is about ₹1,500 per order.
      • Holding cost (H): the cost of keeping one unit in stock for a year. It includes interest on the money blocked in stock, warehouse rent, insurance, damage, pilferage and expiry. It is usually expressed as a percentage of the item's cost per year. Dhanvanshi uses 24% a year, so for a pack costing ₹140, H = 0.24 × 140 = ₹33.60 per pack per year.
      • Stock-out cost: the loss when stock is not available: the lost margin on that sale, and the risk that the customer shifts to another store. This cost is hard to measure, but it is real. We deal with it in Chapter 5.

      The sawtooth pattern

      When an order of Q packs arrives, stock jumps up by Q. It then falls day by day as the branches draw stock, until the next order arrives. The stock level never goes below the safety stock when demand is as expected. On average, half the order quantity is in stock at any time, plus the safety stock.

      Average inventoryAverage inventory = Q2 + Safety stock

      The trade-off between ordering and holding

      If Madavi Warehouse orders small quantities, it places many orders in a year, so ordering cost is high but average stock (and holding cost) is low. If it orders large quantities, it places few orders, but holds more stock. The chart below shows both costs for toor dal, with annual demand D = 120 × 365 = 43,800 packs.

      Annual ordering cost and annual holding cost of cycle stockOrdering cost = DQ × S     Holding cost = Q2 × H

      The total cost is lowest where the two curves cross. This quantity is called the Economic Order Quantity, which we calculate in Chapter 6. The cost of holding safety stock (safety stock × H) is added on top; it does not change with Q.

      Chapter 4

      Lead time and Minimum Order Quantity

      Two facts about the supplier shape every ordering decision: how long it takes to get the stock, and how little we are allowed to order.

      What is lead time?

      Lead time is the time from the moment an order is raised to the moment the stock is received, checked and ready to issue or sell. It is not only the travel time of the truck. For Madavi Warehouse ordering toor dal from Kavya Distributors, the lead time has four parts:

      Lead-time demandLead-time demand = Average daily demand (d) × Lead time in days (L)

      Lead-time demand is the stock needed to keep selling while we wait for the order to arrive. If the stock in hand is less than the lead-time demand when we place the order, the shelf will go empty before the goods arrive.

      Lead time is not fixed

      A truck gets stuck at a toll plaza, the supplier is short of stock, or the GRN waits because the warehouse is busy. Lead time varies from order to order. A planner should look at the actual lead times of the last ten or twenty orders, not the time promised by the supplier. We use this variation in Chapter 5.

      Minimum Order Quantity (MoQ)

      MoQ is the smallest quantity a supplier will accept in one order. Suppliers set an MoQ because each order has a fixed cost for them too: processing, picking, loading and transport. A mill may also produce in batches. Along with the MoQ, most goods move in case packs (cartons or bags): toor dal comes in cartons of 20 packs, so an order must be a multiple of 20.

      Order quantity after MoQ and case packOrder quantity = larger of { Required quantity rounded up to full cases,  MoQ }

      When the requirement is smaller than the MoQ, the extra quantity comes into stock and blocks money until it is sold. For fast-moving items this hardly matters. For slow-moving items, the MoQ can create months of stock.

      What can a buyer do about a high MoQ?

      Ask for the MoQ to be applied on the total value of the order across several items (a mixed-SKU MoQ), buy together with another branch or buyer, accept the MoQ only for items with a long shelf life, or buy the slow item through a stockist such as Nithansh Stockists, who can supply smaller lots at a slightly higher price.

      Chapter 5

      Service level, stock-outs and safety stock

      Demand is never exactly 120 packs a day. Some days it is 90, some days 160. Safety stock protects the shelf on the busy days, and the service level decides how much protection we buy.

      Stock-outs and their cost

      A stock-out happens when a customer wants an item and it is not available. In a store, the customer may buy another brand, come back later, or go to another shop. The last case is the costliest: we lose the sale, and we may lose the customer's future visits. A branch that often runs out of staples like toor dal quickly gets a reputation for it.

      Two ways to measure service

      • Cycle service level (CSL): the percentage of order cycles in which there is no stock-out. A CSL of 95% means that in 95 out of 100 replenishment cycles, stock does not run out before the next order arrives.
      • Fill rate: the percentage of customer demand met from stock. If customers asked for 43,800 packs in a year and 43,650 were supplied, the fill rate is 43,650 ÷ 43,800 = 99.7%.

      Fill rate is usually much higher than CSL, because a stock-out in a cycle often lasts only a few hours at the end of the cycle. In this module, "service level" means the cycle service level.

      Safety stock

      If daily demand varies with a standard deviation of σd, then demand over a lead time of L days varies with a standard deviation of σd × √L (assuming each day's demand is independent of the others). Safety stock is a multiple of this spread. The multiple is the Z value of the chosen service level, read from the standard normal table.

      Safety stockSafety stock (SS) = Z × σd × √L
      Service level80%85%90%95%97%98%99%99.9%
      Z0.841.041.281.651.882.052.333.09

      For toor dal at Madavi Warehouse, d = 120 packs, σd = 24 packs and L = 5 days. At 95%, SS = 1.65 × 24 × √5 = 88.5, which we round up to 89 packs. Always round safety stock up.

      Why the cost rises so fast

      Going from 90% to 95% needs 29% more safety stock. Going from 95% to 99% needs another 41%, and from 99% to 99.9% another 33%. The last few percent of service are the most expensive. That is why stores keep a high service level for staples and high-value items, and a lower one for slow, low-margin items.

      When lead time also varies

      If the supplier's lead time is uncertain, with a standard deviation of σL days, the safety stock must cover both sources of variation.

      Safety stock with variable demand and variable lead timeSS = Z × √( L × σd2 + d2 × σL2 )

      If Kavya's deliveries vary by just one day (σL = 1), SS = 1.65 × √(5 × 242 + 1202 × 12) = 1.65 × √17,280 = 217 packs. That is more than double the 89 packs needed for demand variation alone. An unreliable supplier costs a lot of extra stock.

      Chapter 6

      Reorder point and replenishment

      We now have all the pieces to answer the two questions of replenishment: when to order, and how much to order.

      When to order: the reorder point

      The reorder point (ROP) is the stock level at which a new order must be placed. It is enough to cover the lead-time demand, plus the safety stock for days of higher demand.

      Reorder pointROP = (d × L) + SS = (120 × 5) + 89 = 689 packs

      When comparing stock with the ROP, use the inventory position, not just the stock on the shelf. If an order is already on the way, it must be counted, or the planner will order twice.

      Inventory positionInventory position = Stock in hand + Stock on order − Pending customer orders (backorders)

      How much to order: Economic Order Quantity

      In Chapter 3 we saw that ordering cost falls and holding cost rises as the order quantity increases. The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) is the quantity at which the total of the two is lowest.

      Economic Order QuantityEOQ = √2 × D × SH = √2 × 43,800 × 1,50033.60 = 1,977.6 packs

      Rounded up to full cartons of 20, Madavi Warehouse orders 1,980 packs (99 cartons), about 22 orders a year, or one order every 16 to 17 days. Kavya's MoQ for toor dal is 500 packs, which is below the EOQ, so the MoQ does not change anything here. If the EOQ were below the MoQ, we would order the MoQ.

      EOQ is a guide, not a rule

      EOQ assumes steady demand, a fixed price and unlimited space. The total cost curve is flat near the EOQ, so ordering 1,800 or 2,200 packs costs only slightly more. Planners adjust the EOQ to fit truck capacity, storage space, shelf life and quantity discounts.

      Chapter 7

      Demand forecasting for inventory

      So far we have taken daily demand as 120 packs. In practice, this number comes from a forecast. A better forecast means less safety stock and fewer stock-outs.

      Twelve months of sales

      The table shows monthly sales of toor dal (1 kg) across all three Dhanvanshi branches for the financial year 2025-26. Notice the jump in October, the Dussehra and Diwali month.

      Three simple methods

      In the formulas, At is the actual sale in month t and Ft is the forecast for month t.

      Moving average of the last n monthsFt+1 = At + At−1 + … + At−n+1n
      Weighted moving average (3 months)Ft+1 = 0.5 × At + 0.3 × At−1 + 0.2 × At−2
      Simple exponential smoothingFt+1 = α × At + (1 − α) × Ft   where 0 < α < 1, and the first forecast is the first actual sale

      A moving average gives equal weight to the last n months. A weighted moving average gives more weight to recent months. Exponential smoothing gives weight to all past months, with the weight falling as the months get older. A higher α means the forecast follows recent sales more closely.

      Measuring forecast error

      Error in month t is et = At − Ft. We compare the methods over the same six months, October to March.

      Error measures over n monthsMAE = Σ |et|n     MAPE = Σ |et| / Atn × 100     Bias = Σ etn

      MAE is the average size of the error in packs. MAPE is the average error as a percentage of sales, useful for comparing items of different sizes. Bias shows the direction: a positive bias means the forecast is usually too low (risk of stock-outs); a negative bias means it is usually too high (risk of excess stock).

      No simple method can predict Diwali

      All three methods miss the October jump, and then over-forecast November because they are still reacting to October. Methods based only on the last few months cannot know about a festival. In practice, planners take the base forecast from these methods and add a festival uplift based on last year. Last October, sales were about 23% above the April to September average, so the planner would add a similar uplift for this October and build anticipation stock in September.

      From a monthly forecast to the reorder point

      A monthly forecast of 3,660 packs means about 3,660 ÷ 30 = 122 packs a day. This daily figure is the d in the lead-time demand and reorder point. The standard deviation of the forecast errors is used as σd in the safety stock formula: the more accurate the forecast, the smaller the safety stock.

      The same calculation in Python

      import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

sales = pd.Series([3480, 3560, 3420, 3590, 3650, 3610, 4380, 3930, 3640, 3700, 3560, 3760],
                  index=pd.period_range("2025-04", periods=12, freq="M"))

w = np.array([0.2, 0.3, 0.5])                                   # oldest to latest
ma3  = sales.rolling(3).mean().shift(1)                         # moving average, n = 3
wma3 = sales.rolling(3).apply(lambda x: (x * w).sum(), raw=True).shift(1)
ses  = sales.ewm(alpha=0.3, adjust=False).mean().shift(1)       # exponential smoothing

for name, f in [("MA", ma3), ("WMA", wma3), ("SES", ses)]:
    e = (sales - f).iloc[-6:]                                    # October to March
    print(name, "MAE", round(e.abs().mean()), "MAPE", round((e.abs() / sales.iloc[-6:]).mean() * 100, 1),
          "Bias", round(e.mean()))

      Chapter 8

      Inventory analysis: days of inventory, turnover and ABC

      Planning tells us what to order. Analysis tells us whether the stock we hold is working for the business, or just sitting in the warehouse.

      Inventory turnover and days of inventory

      Stock is valued at cost, so we compare it with the cost of goods sold (COGS), not with sales revenue.

      Average inventoryAverage inventory = Opening inventory + Closing inventory2
      Inventory turnoverInventory turnover = COGS for the periodAverage inventory
      Days of inventory (DOI), also called days of inventory on handDOI = Average inventoryCOGS for the period × Days in the period = Days in the periodInventory turnover

      Turnover tells us how many times the stock is sold and replaced in a period. DOI tells us how many days the current stock would last at the normal rate of consumption. A higher turnover (lower DOI) means less money blocked in stock, as long as stock-outs do not go up.

      DOI and days of cover

      DOI looks back: it uses past COGS. Days of cover (Chapter 2) looks ahead: it divides current stock by forecast demand. Both are in days, and both are useful. Before a festival, days of cover is the better measure, since past consumption understates the coming demand.

      ABC analysis

      A warehouse may stock hundreds of items, and the planner cannot give equal attention to all. ABC analysis ranks items by their annual consumption value (annual units × unit cost) and puts them into three classes. It follows the Pareto principle: a few items account for most of the value.

      • A items: roughly the top 70% of value, usually 10 to 25% of items. Tight control, frequent review, accurate forecasts.
      • B items: the next 20% of value. Normal control.
      • C items: the last 10% of value, often half the items. Simple rules, larger lots, occasional review.

      Method: sort items by annual consumption value, largest first. Work out each item's share of the total and the cumulative share. An item goes into class A if the cumulative share before it is below the A cut-off, and into class B if the cumulative share before it is below the B cut-off. The rest are class C.

      The table shows 20 items at Madavi Warehouse. Annual value and stock are in ₹ lakh, at cost. DOI for each item is its current stock value divided by its average daily consumption value.

      Slow-moving stock hides in class C

      Class C items are small in value, so nobody watches them, and that is where DOI grows. Hing is flagged because of the high MoQ we saw in Chapter 4. Check DOI item by item, not only for the whole warehouse. The overall DOI of about 30 days looks healthy even though some items have more than three months of stock.

      Many companies combine ABC with an XYZ classification based on how steady the demand is (X steady, Y variable, Z very irregular). An AX item such as toor dal can run on tight automatic reorder points, while a CZ item may be ordered only against confirmed demand.

      The same analysis in Python

      import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# df has columns: item, annual_value, stock_value   (values in Rs lakh)
df = df.sort_values("annual_value", ascending=False)
df["share"]   = df["annual_value"] / df["annual_value"].sum() * 100
df["cum_pct"] = df["share"].cumsum()
before = df["cum_pct"] - df["share"]                            # cumulative share before the item
df["class"] = np.select([before < 70, before < 90], ["A", "B"], default="C")
df["doi"]   = df["stock_value"] / (df["annual_value"] / 365)
print(df[df["doi"] > 60])                                       # slow-moving items

      Capstone: replenishment plan for Madavi Warehouse

      It is Monday morning at Madavi Warehouse. The planner must decide which of five items to order from Kavya Distributors today, and how much. Every value in the white boxes can be changed.

      Steps followed for each item

      1. Safety stock: SS = Z × σd × √L, rounded up.
      2. Reorder point: ROP = d × L + SS.
      3. Inventory position: IP = Stock in hand + Stock on order.
      4. If IP ≤ ROP, place an order. Otherwise, no order today.
      5. Order quantity: EOQ = √(2DS / H) with D = 365 × d, then the larger of EOQ and MoQ, rounded up to full cases.
      6. Stock just before the order arrives = IP − d × L. If this is negative, the item will run out before the delivery. Ask for an urgent delivery.
      Service level:

      Ordering cost S = ₹1,500 per order. Holding cost = 24% of unit cost per year.
